Automation's hardest skill isn't answering. It's knowing when to stop.

Four hard stops. These belong to a human immediately, regardless of how confident the system is.

An angry customer. Anger is not a support ticket, it is a relationship problem.

Anything about money in dispute. Refunds under argument, chargebacks, double charges.

Damaged or missing goods. Somebody has lost something and that needs a person.

Anything ambiguous. If confidence is low, stop. Do not have a go.

And a fifth that overrides everything: if they asked for a human, that is the whole test. Nothing else needs checking.

The part that gets built badly is the handoff itself. A handoff without context is just a transfer, and the worst experience in support is explaining the whole thing twice. If the human starts from "how can I help", the automation made things worse rather than better.

What should travel with the conversation: the full history, the order it relates to, what was already checked and ruled out, and why it escalated.

Worth being clear about the position here, because it is the opposite of the one people expect. This is the pro human argument. The value is not a person doing fewer things. It is a person arriving at the four conversations that were always worth their attention, already briefed, instead of reaching the angry one at 5pm after a day of sizing questions.

Write your handoff policy before you automate anything, even if the automation is a holiday autoresponder. The list of things you will never let software handle is the most useful support document you will write this year.
